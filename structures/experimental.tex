\subsection{Dataset Description and Experimental Setup}

The empirical foundation of this thesis rests on a synthetic smart manufacturing sensor dataset comprising 100,000 timestamped observations generated to simulate a group of 50 industrial machines over a continuous 69-day monitoring period spanning from January 1, 2025 at 00:00:00 to March 11, 2025 at 10:39:00. Each observation records the simultaneous state of thirteen variables: a timestamp, a machine identifier (ranging from 1 to 50), five continuous sensor channels (temperature, vibration, humidity, pressure, and energy consumption), three derived condition indicators (machine status, anomaly flag, and downtime risk), and three maintenance-relevant labels (predicted remaining useful life, failure type, and maintenance required). The dataset is complete with no missing values across all 1,300,000 individual data points. The synthetic data generation process incorporates realistic sensor correlations, anomaly patterns, and failure modes representative of actual industrial predictive maintenance scenarios, providing a controlled environment for evaluating the QASAMAP framework's performance.

The dataset directly operationalizes three distinct experimental pipelines implemented across the three QASAMAP code components. In \texttt{tcgn\_updated.py}, the full 100,000-observation dataset is loaded, feature-engineered, and partitioned into per-machine training (80\%) and test (20\%) splits for T-GCN and comparative model training and evaluation. In \texttt{agenticai.py}, a representative five-event sample from Machine 40 spanning 10:40:00 to 10:44:00 on March 11, 2025 serves as the streaming inference demonstration. In \texttt{quantum\_agent.py}, a multi-machine sample of 11 events covering machines 1, 2, 3, 4, 32, 40, and 47 provides the basis for the full quantum-agentic pipeline demonstration and three-way comparative analysis. All experiments were executed on a server equipped with an NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB GPU, which provided the computational environment for deep learning model training in the forecasting and classification experiments.

The experimental evaluation is organized into four distinct components: (1) spatiotemporal forecasting model comparison across T-GCN and LSTM-based architectures; (2) machine health classification and regression across five supervised learning algorithms; (3) agentic AI pipeline evaluation without quantum enhancement; and (4) agentic AI pipeline evaluation with full quantum computing integration. Together, these four components provide a complete empirical characterisation of the QASAMAP framework, enabling both component-level analysis and full-system performance attribution.

\subsection{Forecasting Model Experiment}

The first experimental component evaluates spatiotemporal sensor forecasting performance across three architectures: a standalone Optimized LSTM baseline, the proposed T-GCN with cross-correlation adjacency (CC-Adj), and a Hybrid LSTM-GCN combining both architectures. All models were trained and evaluated on the 100,000-observation dataset partitioned into 80/20 per-machine train/test splits. The cross-correlation graph for T-GCN comprises 50 nodes (machines) and 538 directed edges constructed using the \texttt{compute\_machine\_lag\_matrix} function in \texttt{tcgn\_updated.py}. Temperature was selected as the cross-correlation feature for graph construction because: (1) thermal dynamics exhibit the strongest cross-machine coupling in industrial environments due to shared ambient conditions and HVAC systems; (2) temperature anomalies often precede other sensor anomalies, providing early warning signals; and (3) the continuous, smooth nature of temperature trajectories yields more reliable lag estimates than discrete or noisy channels such as pressure. Machine 40 was identified as the anchor node based on its highest out-degree centrality, with key top influencer machines being 26, 38, 42, 47, and 14, and key top dependent machines being 29, 40, 28, 8, and 16. Training was performed on an NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB GPU. The key hyperparameters for the T-GCN model are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters_tgcn}.

The T-GCN with cross-correlation adjacency achieves the highest average R\textsuperscript{2} of 0.7702, representing a 12.4\% improvement over the OptimizedLSTM baseline (R\textsuperscript{2} = 0.6853) and an 8.0\% improvement over the Hybrid LSTM-GCN (R\textsuperscript{2} = 0.7134). The improvement is most pronounced for humidity prediction (R\textsuperscript{2} = 0.9080 vs.\ 0.8761 for LSTM) and energy consumption (R\textsuperscript{2} = 0.6581 vs.\ 0.5522), where the graph-based propagation of correlated cross-machine signals provides the greatest benefit. The pressure sensor channel remains the most challenging to forecast across all three models, with R\textsuperscript{2} values ranging from 0.4254 to 0.5002, reflecting the lower unique-value count (401 distinct values) and the more complex, possibly setpoint-driven dynamics of pressure in the monitored machine pool.

Although the T-GCN requires a longer training time (111.28 seconds vs.\ 52.56 seconds for the standalone LSTM), this overhead is attributable to the graph convolution operations over 538 edges during each forward pass and is acceptable given the significant performance gains. The Hybrid LSTM-GCN achieves intermediate R\textsuperscript{2} performance (0.7134) with the shortest training time (47.89 seconds), offering a practical trade-off when computational resources are constrained.

\subsubsection{Forecasting Experiment Visualisations}

\paragraph{Cross-Correlation Strength Matrix.}
Figure~\ref{fig:cc_matrix} presents the pairwise cross-correlation strength matrix computed from the temperature time series of the first 20 machines in the dataset. Each cell $(i, j)$ encodes the absolute Pearson correlation between machine $i$ and machine $j$ at the lag of maximum correlation, computed by \texttt{compute\_machine\_lag\_matrix} in \texttt{tcgn\_updated.py}. The diagonal entries are identically 1.0 (each machine is perfectly correlated with itself), while off-diagonal entries reveal the inter-machine temperature coupling structure that defines the T-GCN adjacency graph. The predominance of light-yellow and light-orange cells indicates that most machine pairs exhibit weak to negligible direct linear coupling ($|r| < 0.35$), consistent with independent production lines operating under shared environmental conditions. Scattered deeper-orange and red cells identify specific machine pairs --- notably machines 3--13, 17--19, and 19--20 --- with moderate negative correlations, suggesting thermal counter-cycling patterns in which one machine's heat signature inversely predicts another's at a characteristic lag. Only machine pairs with $|r| > 0.35$ are retained as directed edges in the T-GCN graph, yielding the 538 edges that constitute the spatial backbone of the model.

Figure~\ref{fig:model_performance} presents a four-panel performance summary comparing the three forecasting architectures across R\textsuperscript{2} per feature, MSE per feature, aggregate metrics, and training loss convergence. The top-left panel confirms T-GCN's consistent R\textsuperscript{2} advantage across all seven sensor channels, with the most pronounced improvements observed for vibration (R\textsuperscript{2}: 0.594 vs.\ 0.574 for LSTM) and energy consumption (R\textsuperscript{2}: 0.658 vs.\ 0.552 for LSTM). The top-right MSE panel highlights that the \texttt{temp\_vibe\_id} feature dominates absolute error magnitude due to its large numerical scale (product of temperature and vibration values), while all other sensor channels produce near-zero MSE on the visible scale. The bottom-left aggregate metrics panel clearly shows T-GCN achieving the highest average R\textsuperscript{2} ($\approx$0.77), with the Hybrid LSTM-GCN intermediate ($\approx$0.71) and the standalone LSTM lowest ($\approx$0.69). The bottom-right training loss convergence panel is particularly informative: T-GCN converges most rapidly, dropping steeply between epochs 0 and 4 and stabilising at a final loss of $\approx$0.024, substantially below the OptimizedLSTM final loss of $\approx$0.031 and the Hybrid LSTM-GCN loss of $\approx$0.028. This rapid convergence reflects the graph convolution layers' ability to immediately leverage cross-machine spatial structure from epoch 1, in contrast to the purely temporal LSTM that must build up temporal representations more gradually.

\paragraph{Multi-Horizon Autoregressive Prediction.}
Figure~\ref{fig:multi_horizon} presents a $4 \times 3$ panel grid showing the autoregressive rollout prediction performance across 10 forecast horizon steps for four sensor channels (temperature, vibration, humidity, pressure) and all three models. Each subplot overlays the actual sensor trajectory (solid black line), the model's predicted trajectory (dashed coloured line), and a shaded $\pm 1\sigma$ uncertainty band computed across multiple starting windows. Several observations are noteworthy. First, all three models track the actual temperature and vibration trajectories closely at short horizons (steps 1--3), with divergence increasing beyond step 5 as autoregressive error accumulates. Second, the T-GCN uncertainty bands (gold shading) are generally narrower than those of the OptimizedLSTM (blue shading) for the humidity and pressure channels, indicating more consistent multi-step predictions across different starting windows. Third, the humidity channel shows the strongest overall predictive accuracy across all models, consistent with its highest individual R\textsuperscript{2} scores in the per-feature benchmark. The pressure channel exhibits the largest $\pm 1\sigma$ spread, reflecting the setpoint-driven dynamics that resist smooth autoregressive rollout by all architectures.

\paragraph{Horizon MAE Degradation Curve.}
Figure~\ref{fig:mae_degradation} quantifies forecast error accumulation as a function of prediction horizon. The four subplots present MAE vs.\ horizon step (1--10) for temperature, vibration, humidity, and pressure respectively. A clear pattern of non-monotonic MAE growth is observed across all channels: MAE does not increase uniformly with horizon but instead exhibits local peaks at steps 4--6, followed by partial recovery at longer horizons. This non-monotonic pattern reflects the semi-periodic nature of the sensor dynamics, where the model's autoregressive predictions re-synchronise with the actual trajectory as it returns to its mean. T-GCN consistently achieves the lowest MAE at almost every horizon step across all four channels, with the margin over the LSTM baseline widening at the critical medium-range horizons (steps 5--8) that are most operationally relevant for predictive maintenance scheduling. The vibration channel shows the highest absolute MAE values (ranging from approximately 1.2 to 2.0 across models), confirming it as the most difficult channel for multi-step forecasting, while humidity achieves the lowest MAE despite its wide dynamic range.

\paragraph{Multi-Source Cross-Correlation Graph Simulation.}
Figure~\ref{fig:cc_graph_sim} illustrates the T-GCN's cross-correlation graph propagation mechanism through a multi-source simulation using three source machines (Machine 40, Machine 27, and Machine 14) and three dependent machines (Machine 1, Machine 50, and Machine 32). During the first 50 time steps, the three source machines (dashed lines) exhibit high-amplitude oscillations between approximately 50 and 100 units, representing normal operating variability in the simulated sensor feature. The dependent machines (solid lines) mirror these oscillations with a characteristic lag, visually demonstrating the propagation of cross-machine influence through the graph edges. At approximately time step 50, all traces collapse to near-zero, simulating the joint effect of a coordinated anomaly or shutdown event initiated at the source machines and propagated to their dependents. This simulation validates the \texttt{propagate\_from\_anchor} function in \texttt{tcgn\_updated.py} and demonstrates that the T-GCN's graph structure successfully encodes the directional influence relationships identified in the cross-correlation lag matrix, enabling the model to anticipate downstream machine behaviour from upstream source signals.

\subsection{Mathematical Formulations of Forecasting Models}

\subsubsection{Cross-Correlation Lag Graph Construction}

The T-GCN spatial backbone is a directed weighted graph $G = (V, E, W)$ constructed from pairwise lagged cross-correlations of the temperature time series. For each ordered machine pair $(m_i, m_j)$, the cross-correlation function at integer lag $\tau$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{ij}(\tau) = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T-|\tau|} \bigl(T_i(t) - \bar{T}_i\bigr)\bigl(T_j(t+\tau) - \bar{T}_j\bigr)}
    {\sqrt{\sum_{t}(T_i(t)-\bar{T}_i)^2}\sqrt{\sum_{t}(T_j(t)-\bar{T}_j)^2}}
    \label{eq:xcorr}
\end{equation}
where $T_i(t)$ is the temperature reading of machine $i$ at timestep $t$, $\bar{T}_i$ is its time-series mean, and $\tau \in \{-10, -9, \ldots, +10\}$. The lag of maximum absolute correlation and its strength are recorded as:
\begin{equation}
    \tau^*_{ij} = \arg\max_{\tau} |\rho_{ij}(\tau)|, \qquad
    s_{ij} = |\rho_{ij}(\tau^*_{ij})|
    \label{eq:best_lag}
\end{equation}
A directed edge $e_{ij}$ with weight $s_{ij}$ is added to $G$ if and only if $s_{ij} > 0.35$. The resulting adjacency matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ (where $N = 50$) is then symmetrically normalised as:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{A} = D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2}
    \label{eq:norm_adj}
\end{equation}
where $D_{ii} = \sum_j A_{ij}$ is the degree matrix. This normalisation ensures that the spectral radius of $\hat{A}$ is bounded by 1, preventing gradient explosion during graph convolution. The construction yields 538 directed edges, with Machine~40 having the highest out-degree centrality $d^+_{40} = \sum_j A_{40j}$.

\subsubsection{Graph Convolutional Network Layer}

Each Graph Convolutional (GCN) layer propagates information across the machine graph by computing a weighted aggregation of neighbouring node features. For layer $\ell$ at timestep $t$, the forward pass is:
\begin{equation}
    H^{(\ell+1)}_t = \sigma\!\left(\hat{A}\, H^{(\ell)}_t\, W^{(\ell)}\right)
    \label{eq:gcn}
\end{equation}
where $H^{(\ell)}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d_\ell}$ is the node feature matrix at layer $\ell$ (with $d_0 = 7$ input features), $W^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell \times d_{\ell+1}}$ is the learnable weight matrix ($d_1 = d_2 = 64$), and $\sigma(\cdot)$ denotes the ReLU activation. Dropout with $p = 0.2$ is applied after each GCN layer. The two-layer GCN produces spatial embeddings $H^{(2)}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 64}$ capturing cross-machine correlations at each timestep.

The spectral motivation for Equation~\ref{eq:gcn} follows from the graph Fourier transform: the normalised adjacency $\hat{A}$ is a first-order approximation of the graph Laplacian filter $\tilde{L} = I - \hat{A}$, and applying $\hat{A}$ to node features is equivalent to low-pass filtering on the graph spectral domain, smoothing feature values across strongly correlated machine neighbours.

\subsubsection{Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) Temporal Layer}

The GRU layer processes the sequence of GCN-encoded spatial embeddings $\{H^{(2)}_1, H^{(2)}_2, \ldots, H^{(2)}_W\}$ (window length $W = 10$) to model temporal evolution. For each machine node independently, the GRU update equations at timestep $t$ are:
\begin{align}
    z_t &= \sigma_g\!\left(W_z x_t + U_z h_{t-1} + b_z\right)  \label{eq:gru_update} \\
    r_t &= \sigma_g\!\left(W_r x_t + U_r h_{t-1} + b_r\right)  \label{eq:gru_reset} \\
    \tilde{h}_t &= \tanh\!\left(W_h x_t + U_h (r_t \odot h_{t-1}) + b_h\right)  \label{eq:gru_candidate} \\
    h_t &= (1 - z_t) \odot h_{t-1} + z_t \odot \tilde{h}_t  \label{eq:gru_hidden}
\end{align}
where $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{64}$ is the GCN embedding at time $t$, $h_{t-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{128}$ is the previous hidden state, $z_t$ is the update gate controlling how much of the previous state is retained, $r_t$ is the reset gate controlling how much past information is forgotten, $\tilde{h}_t$ is the candidate hidden state, and $\odot$ denotes elementwise multiplication. $\sigma_g(\cdot)$ is the sigmoid function. Two stacked GRU layers with hidden size 128 are used, with Dropout 0.2 applied between layers.

The update gate $z_t$ implements an adaptive interpolation between the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$ (long-term temporal memory) and the candidate $\tilde{h}_t$ (new information), which is critical for industrial sensor modelling where degradation occurs over multiple time scales simultaneously.

\subsubsection{T-GCN Training Objective and Backpropagation}

The T-GCN is trained to minimise the mean squared error between predicted and actual next-step differenced sensor values over all machines and sensor channels:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}} = \frac{1}{N \cdot F \cdot |\mathcal{B}|} \sum_{b \in \mathcal{B}} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{f=1}^{F}
    \left(\hat{y}^{(b)}_{i,f} - y^{(b)}_{i,f}\right)^2
    \label{eq:tgcn_loss}
\end{equation}
where $N = 50$ is the number of machines, $F = 7$ is the number of sensor channels (including engineered features), $\mathcal{B}$ is the mini-batch, $\hat{y}^{(b)}_{i,f}$ is the model prediction, and $y^{(b)}_{i,f}$ is the ground-truth value. The loss is backpropagated through time (BPTT) across the GRU unrolling and through the GCN graph structure using the normalised adjacency gradient:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W^{(\ell)}} = \sum_{t=1}^{W} \left(H^{(\ell)}_t\right)^T \hat{A}^T \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial H^{(\ell+1)}_t}
    \label{eq:gcn_grad}
\end{equation}
Optimisation uses Adam with learning rate $\eta = 5 \times 10^{-4}$, weight decay $\lambda = 10^{-5}$, and cosine annealing schedule $\eta_t = \eta_{\min} + \frac{1}{2}(\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\min})(1 + \cos(\pi t / T))$. Gradient clipping at $\|\nabla\|_2 \leq 1.0$ prevents instability during BPTT. The two engineered input features are defined as:
\begin{equation}
    x_{\text{temp\_vibe}} = T_i \times V_i, \qquad
    x_{\text{press\_energy}} = \frac{E_i}{P_i + 0.1}
    \label{eq:eng_feat}
\end{equation}
where $T_i$, $V_i$, $E_i$, $P_i$ are temperature, vibration, energy, and pressure for machine $i$ respectively.

\subsubsection{LSTM Baseline Formulation}

The OptimizedLSTM baseline processes each machine's sensor sequence independently without graph structure. The LSTM cell equations at timestep $t$ are:
\begin{align}
    f_t &= \sigma_g(W_f [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f)  \label{eq:lstm_forget} \\
    i_t &= \sigma_g(W_i [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_i)  \label{eq:lstm_input} \\
    o_t &= \sigma_g(W_o [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_o)  \label{eq:lstm_output} \\
    \tilde{c}_t &= \tanh(W_c [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_c)  \label{eq:lstm_candidate} \\
    c_t &= f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{c}_t  \label{eq:lstm_cell} \\
    h_t &= o_t \odot \tanh(c_t)  \label{eq:lstm_hidden}
\end{align}
where $f_t$, $i_t$, $o_t$ are the forget, input, and output gates respectively, $c_t$ is the cell state carrying long-range temporal information, and $\tilde{c}_t$ is the candidate cell update. The distinction between $c_t$ (additive cell state) and $h_t$ (gated output) enables LSTMs to model long-range dependencies more effectively than vanilla RNNs by allowing gradients to flow through the additive cell state path with minimal attenuation.

The Hybrid LSTM-GCN concatenates the GCN spatial embedding $H^{(2)}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 64}$ with the LSTM hidden state $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 128}$ before the output head:
\begin{equation}
    z_{\text{hybrid},t} = [H^{(2)}_t \,\|\, h_t] \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 192}
    \label{eq:hybrid}
\end{equation}
where $[\cdot \| \cdot]$ denotes feature-dimension concatenation. The output head maps this to predictions: Linear$(192 \to 64)$ + ReLU + Linear$(64 \to 7)$. This naive concatenation, while computationally efficient, does not allow spatial and temporal information to interact during the recurrent computation — a limitation compared to T-GCN's integrated spatial-temporal propagation.

\subsubsection{Forecasting Evaluation Metrics}

For each model and sensor channel $f$, the following metrics are computed on the held-out test split (20\% per machine):
\begin{align}
    \text{MSE}_f &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\hat{y}_{k,f} - y_{k,f})^2
    \label{eq:mse} \\[4pt]
    \text{RMSE}_f &= \sqrt{\text{MSE}_f}
    \label{eq:rmse} \\[4pt]
    \text{MAE}_f &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}|\hat{y}_{k,f} - y_{k,f}|
    \label{eq:mae} \\[4pt]
    R^2_f &= 1 - \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\hat{y}_{k,f} - y_{k,f})^2}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}(y_{k,f} - \bar{y}_f)^2}
    \label{eq:r2}
\end{align}
where $n$ is the number of test samples and $\bar{y}_f$ is the test-set mean. The average R$^2$ across all $F$ channels is $\overline{R^2} = \frac{1}{F}\sum_{f=1}^{F} R^2_f$. An R$^2$ of 1.0 indicates perfect prediction; R$^2 = 0$ means the model performs no better than predicting the mean; negative R$^2$ indicates predictions worse than the mean predictor.

\subsection{Mathematical Formulations of Classification and Regression Models}

\subsubsection{Gradient Boosting Objective and Tree Construction}

All gradient boosting models (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) share a common additive modelling framework. An ensemble of $K$ decision trees produces a prediction:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_i = \sum_{k=1}^{K} f_k(\mathbf{x}_i), \quad f_k \in \mathcal{F}
    \label{eq:gb_ensemble}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}$ is the space of regression trees. The regularised objective at boosting round $t$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}^{(t)} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell\!\left(y_i,\, \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)} + f_t(\mathbf{x}_i)\right) + \Omega(f_t)
    \label{eq:xgb_obj}
\end{equation}
where $\ell(y_i, \hat{y}_i)$ is the task-specific loss (cross-entropy for classification, MSE for regression) and the regularisation term is $\Omega(f_t) = \gamma T + \frac{1}{2}\lambda \|w\|^2$, with $T$ the number of leaves, $w$ the leaf weight vector, $\gamma$ the minimum leaf gain, and $\lambda$ the $\ell_2$ leaf weight penalty. Taking a second-order Taylor expansion around $\hat{y}^{(t-1)}_i$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}^{(t)} \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ g_i f_t(\mathbf{x}_i) + \frac{1}{2} h_i f_t(\mathbf{x}_i)^2 \right] + \Omega(f_t)
    \label{eq:xgb_taylor}
\end{equation}
where $g_i = \partial_{\hat{y}} \ell(y_i, \hat{y}^{(t-1)}_i)$ is the first-order gradient (residual) and $h_i = \partial^2_{\hat{y}} \ell(y_i, \hat{y}^{(t-1)}_i)$ is the second-order Hessian. For binary cross-entropy loss $\ell(y,\hat{p}) = -y\log\hat{p} - (1-y)\log(1-\hat{p})$, these simplify to $g_i = \hat{p}_i - y_i$ and $h_i = \hat{p}_i(1-\hat{p}_i)$.

The optimal leaf weight for leaf $j$ containing instance set $I_j$ is obtained by setting the derivative of Equation~\ref{eq:xgb_taylor} to zero:
\begin{equation}
    w_j^* = -\frac{\sum_{i \in I_j} g_i}{\sum_{i \in I_j} h_i + \lambda}
    \label{eq:optimal_leaf}
\end{equation}
The corresponding optimal gain for splitting leaf $j$ into left child $I_L$ and right child $I_R$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Gain} = \frac{1}{2}\left[
        \frac{\left(\sum_{i \in I_L} g_i\right)^2}{\sum_{i \in I_L} h_i + \lambda}
        + \frac{\left(\sum_{i \in I_R} g_i\right)^2}{\sum_{i \in I_R} h_i + \lambda}
        - \frac{\left(\sum_{i \in I_j} g_i\right)^2}{\sum_{i \in I_j} h_i + \lambda}
    \right] - \gamma
    \label{eq:split_gain}
\end{equation}
A split is executed if Gain $> 0$. Equation~\ref{eq:split_gain} shows explicitly that $\lambda$ regularises the gain by reducing the numerator weight of each child relative to the denominator, shrinking leaf weights toward zero and preventing overfitting on small leaf populations such as the minority failure classes.

\subsubsection{LightGBM: Histogram-Based Splitting and GOSS}

LightGBM accelerates tree construction through histogram-based split finding, which discretises continuous feature values into $B = 255$ bins and accumulates gradient statistics per bin rather than sorting individual values. For feature $f$ and bin $b$, the bin-level gradient and Hessian sums are:
\begin{equation}
    G_b^f = \sum_{i: x_{if} \in \text{bin}_b} g_i, \qquad H_b^f = \sum_{i: x_{if} \in \text{bin}_b} h_i
    \label{eq:lgbm_hist}
\end{equation}
The split gain for threshold $b$ is then computed from prefix sums of $G_b^f$ and $H_b^f$, reducing the split search from $\mathcal{O}(n)$ per feature to $\mathcal{O}(B)$. This reduces the computational complexity of tree construction from $\mathcal{O}(nF\log n)$ to $\mathcal{O}(BF\log n)$.

Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS) further accelerates training by retaining all instances with large gradient magnitudes (top fraction $a = 0.2$) and randomly sampling a fraction $b = 0.1$ of small-gradient instances, weighting the sampled instances by $\frac{1-a}{b}$ to correct for the introduced sampling bias:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{G}_b^f = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{A}: x_{if} \in \text{bin}_b} g_i +
                   \frac{1-a}{b} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}: x_{if} \in \text{bin}_b} g_i
    \label{eq:goss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}$ is the large-gradient set and $\mathcal{B}$ is the sampled small-gradient set. GOSS ensures that the most informative training instances (those with large residuals, typically corresponding to difficult minority-class examples) are always included in gradient estimation, making it particularly effective for the class-imbalanced failure type classification task.

\subsubsection{CatBoost: Ordered Boosting and Symmetric Trees}

CatBoost addresses target leakage in gradient estimation --- a subtle bias in standard boosting where gradient statistics for instance $i$ are computed using a model that was partially trained on $i$ itself --- through ordered boosting. For each training instance $i$, the gradient is computed against a model $M_{\sigma(i)}$ trained only on instances $\{j : \sigma(j) < \sigma(i)\}$ in a random permutation $\sigma$:
\begin{equation}
    g_i^{\text{ordered}} = \frac{\partial \ell(y_i, M_{\sigma(i)}(\mathbf{x}_i))}{\partial M_{\sigma(i)}(\mathbf{x}_i)}
    \label{eq:catboost_ordered}
\end{equation}
This unbiased gradient estimate is particularly important for the RUL regression task, where target leakage can artificially inflate training-set performance without improving generalisation.

CatBoost also constructs symmetric (oblivious) trees in which the same split condition is applied at all nodes of the same depth:
\begin{equation}
    \text{tree}(\mathbf{x}) = w_{b(x,d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_D)}
    \label{eq:symtree}
\end{equation}
where $b(\mathbf{x}, d_1, \ldots, d_D)$ is the leaf index determined by $D$ split decisions applied uniformly at each depth, and $w$ is the corresponding leaf weight. Symmetric trees provide regularisation through their constrained structure and enable fast inference via binary indexing of leaf weights.

\subsubsection{TabNet: Sequential Attention and Sparse Feature Selection}

TabNet processes tabular features through a multi-step sequential attention mechanism. At each step $s$, an attention transformer selects a sparse subset of features via a learned mask $M[s] \in [0,1]^{B \times F}$ (where $B$ is batch size and $F$ is number of features):
\begin{equation}
    M[s] = \text{sparsemax}\!\left(P[s] \cdot h_{\text{att}}(a[s])\right)
    \label{eq:tabnet_mask}
\end{equation}
where $h_{\text{att}}(\cdot)$ is a fully connected layer, $a[s]$ is the processed feature at step $s$, and $P[s]$ is a prior scale term penalising features already selected in previous steps:
\begin{equation}
    P[s] = \prod_{s'=1}^{s-1}\!\left(\gamma - M[s']\right), \quad P[1] = \mathbf{1}
    \label{eq:tabnet_prior}
\end{equation}
The hyperparameter $\gamma \geq 1$ controls the degree of feature reuse across steps; $\gamma = 1$ enforces exclusive feature selection at each step, while $\gamma > 1$ allows gradual reuse. The masked features are fed into a feature transformer $h_{\text{feat}}(\cdot)$ to produce step output $d[s]$. The final prediction aggregates outputs across all $N_{\text{steps}}$ steps:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y} = W_{\text{out}} \sum_{s=1}^{N_{\text{steps}}} \text{ReLU}(d[s])
    \label{eq:tabnet_output}
\end{equation}
The aggregate feature importance for input $j$ across the full model is $I_j = \sum_{s,b} M_b[s]_j \big/ (B \cdot N_{\text{steps}})$, providing built-in interpretability. TabNet's consistently lower performance compared to gradient boosting in this experiment is attributable to the relatively low dimensionality of the input features ($F = 5$ raw sensors plus a small set of engineered features), where the sequential attention mechanism provides insufficient advantage over the direct split-based feature selection of tree methods.

\subsubsection{Classification Evaluation Metrics}

For each classification target, the following metrics are computed. For multi-class tasks with $C$ classes, all metrics are macro-averaged. Precision and recall for class $c$ are:
\begin{equation}
    P_c = \frac{\text{TP}_c}{\text{TP}_c + \text{FP}_c}, \qquad
    R_c = \frac{\text{TP}_c}{\text{TP}_c + \text{FN}_c}
    \label{eq:pr}
\end{equation}
The F1 score per class and macro-averaged F1 are:
\begin{equation}
    F1_c = \frac{2 P_c R_c}{P_c + R_c}, \qquad
    \overline{F1} = \frac{1}{C} \sum_{c=1}^{C} F1_c
    \label{eq:f1}
\end{equation}
The AUC-ROC for binary classification is the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve traced by varying the decision threshold $\tau$ over $[0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
    \text{AUC} = \int_0^1 \text{TPR}(\text{FPR}^{-1}(t))\, dt = P(\hat{p}_{+} > \hat{p}_{-})
    \label{eq:auc}
\end{equation}
where the second equality (the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney statistic interpretation) shows that AUC equals the probability that a randomly selected positive instance receives a higher predicted probability than a randomly selected negative instance, making it threshold-independent. For multi-class tasks, AUC is computed using the one-vs-rest scheme.

The Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) provides a balanced summary for imbalanced classes:
\begin{equation}
    \text{MCC} = \frac{\text{TP} \cdot \text{TN} - \text{FP} \cdot \text{FN}}
    {\sqrt{(\text{TP}+\text{FP})(\text{TP}+\text{FN})(\text{TN}+\text{FP})(\text{TN}+\text{FN})}}
    \label{eq:mcc}
\end{equation}
MCC $= +1$ indicates perfect classification; MCC $= 0$ is equivalent to random guessing; MCC $= -1$ is perfect inverse classification. Unlike F1-score, MCC incorporates all four cells of the confusion matrix and is robust to extreme class imbalance, making it the most informative single metric for the failure type classification task where Normal instances constitute 91.90\% of the data.

\subsubsection{RUL Regression Evaluation}

The Remaining Useful Life regression task evaluates predictions $\hat{r}_i$ against ground-truth RUL values $r_i$ (in hours) using:
\begin{align}
    \text{MAE} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} |\hat{r}_i - r_i|
    \label{eq:rul_mae} \\[4pt]
    \text{RMSE} &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{r}_i - r_i)^2}
    \label{eq:rul_rmse} \\[4pt]
    R^2 &= 1 - \frac{\sum_i (\hat{r}_i - r_i)^2}{\sum_i (r_i - \bar{r})^2}
    \label{eq:rul_r2} \\[4pt]
    \text{Explained Variance} &= 1 - \frac{\text{Var}(r - \hat{r})}{\text{Var}(r)}
    \label{eq:ev}
\end{align}
The difference between $R^2$ and Explained Variance is that $R^2$ is sensitive to mean prediction bias: if $\hat{r}_i = r_i + c$ for a constant $c \neq 0$, then $R^2 < \text{EV}$. Since tree-based models may exhibit systematic bias in RUL prediction (particularly near the boundaries of the RUL range), reporting both metrics provides a more complete characterisation of prediction quality. The near-uniform RUL distribution (mean 234.27 h, std 150.06 h) implies $\text{Var}(r) = 150.06^2 \approx 22{,}518$ h$^2$, so the low $R^2 = 0.189$ for the best model (CatBoost) corresponds to a residual variance of $0.811 \times 22{,}518 \approx 18{,}262$ h$^2$, i.e., a residual standard deviation of approximately 135 hours --- insufficient for operationally precise maintenance planning based on single-snapshot features alone.

\subsection{Summary of Experimental Findings}

The experimental evaluation yields four primary findings. First, the T-GCN architecture with cross-correlation adjacency achieves the best spatiotemporal forecasting performance (avg.\ R\textsuperscript{2} = 0.7702), outperforming both the standalone LSTM (R\textsuperscript{2} = 0.6853) and the Hybrid LSTM-GCN (R\textsuperscript{2} = 0.7134), demonstrating the value of graph-based cross-machine dependency modeling for industrial sensor forecasting.

Second, classical tree-based ensemble models (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) achieve near-perfect accuracy and F1 scores ($>$0.99) on anomaly flag and downtime risk binary classification, reflecting the high discriminability of these targets in the dataset. However, the machine status, failure type, and maintenance required classification tasks present more meaningful challenges, with best F1 scores of 0.71, 0.88, and 0.87 respectively, and the RUL regression task exposes the fundamental limitations of single-snapshot classical models (best R\textsuperscript{2} = 0.19).

Third, the empirical evaluation of the agentic AI layer (Chapter~5) and the quantum computing layer (Chapter~6) is reported in their respective dedicated chapters with explicit pre-registration, statistical tests, and what-can/cannot-be-claimed sections per experiment. Headline findings are E1 capability coverage (Hybrid QASAMAP $55\%$ vs Pure ML $20\%$, Cochran's Q significant on six of eight PdM decision dimensions), E-Q3 entangled detection ($F1 = 0.290$ at natural anomaly prevalence on $N = 4000$, beating its mathematically isolated separable equivalent by $+0.078$ F1), and E-Q1 QAOA-vs-SA scheduling parity at depth $p \ge 2$ (Wilcoxon $p = 0.317$ for $p = 2$ on $N = 20$ paired QUBO instances). All quantum results are obtained on noise-free Qiskit Aer statevector simulators; no claim of quantum advantage on QASAMAP is made.
