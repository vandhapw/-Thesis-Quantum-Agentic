\subsection{Motivation for Quantum-Inspired Classical Optimization}
\label{sec:quantum_integration}

The motivation for incorporating quantum-\emph{inspired} classical optimization into QASAMAP is grounded in two computational challenges revealed by the dataset analysis. The first challenge is multi-sensor risk encoding under class imbalance: the five sensor modalities exhibit complex nonlinear inter-sensor relationships, and the severe failure class imbalance (Electrical Fault: 1,014 instances vs.\ Normal: 91,899) means that minority failure patterns occupy a small, non-convex region of the classical feature space that threshold-based classifiers fail to capture reliably. Quantum-inspired encoding offers a principled \emph{mathematical} mechanism for representing these complex multi-sensor risk combinations as expectation-like values that carry richer information than individual sensor thresholds. The second challenge is the combinatorial complexity of maintenance scheduling: with 50 machines each generating a binary maintenance decision variable and pairwise conflict constraints, the scheduling problem belongs to the NP-hard combinatorial optimization class for which quantum algorithms \emph{may} offer asymptotic advantages at sufficient scale \cite{Blekos2024}.

\textbf{Critical Implementation Disclosure.} All ``quantum'' components described in this section are implemented as \textbf{classical heuristics} in the current QASAMAP codebase (Ver13). The `PauliZRiskEncoder` is implemented as a normalized energy-distance anomaly score; the `QuantumAnnealingOptimizer` is a random constrained local search; and the `QAOAScheduler` is a greedy graph-message-passing scheduler. These heuristics are intentionally designed to mirror the \emph{mathematical structure} of their quantum counterparts (QUBO/Ising formulations, Pauli-Z expectation mappings) so that they can be replaced by genuine quantum circuits when hardware maturity permits. The experimental results reported in Chapter~6 therefore reflect \textbf{classical heuristic performance}, not execution on a quantum processor. This disclosure is made in accordance with the post-audit rewrite (2026-05) that corrected earlier misleading quantum labels.

\subsection{Quantum Foundations: Superposition, Entanglement, and Expectation Values}

Three quantum mechanical principles \emph{underpin the design} of the QASAMAP classical heuristics. Superposition enables a qubit to exist in a continuous linear combination of basis states $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ (where $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$), allowing a quantum circuit to represent a sensor value as a continuous probability amplitude rather than a discrete binary variable. In the \emph{quantum-inspired} `PauliZRiskEncoder` heuristic, a Hadamard gate $H$ creates an equal superposition $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)$, and a subsequent $R_Z(\theta)$ rotation encodes the normalised sensor value as a phase shift, producing a state whose Pauli-Z expectation $\langle Z \rangle = \cos(\theta)$ varies continuously from $+1$ (safe) to $-1$ (danger) as $\theta$ ranges from $0$ to $\pi$. The \emph{classical} implementation computes the same scalar via $\cos(\pi \cdot \text{norm}_i)$ without executing a quantum circuit.

Entanglement, created by CNOT gates coupling adjacent sensor qubits, establishes quantum correlations between sensor modalities such that the composite state of the 5-qubit register cannot be factored into independent single-qubit states. In the `PauliZRiskEncoder` heuristic, four CNOT gates between consecutive qubit pairs $(q_0, q_1)$, $(q_1, q_2)$, $(q_2, q_3)$, $(q_3, q_4)$ capture pairwise inter-sensor correlations --- for example, the joint contribution of simultaneously elevated temperature and vibration to the machine's composite risk level. The classical implementation preserves the same weighted-sum formula but does not execute entangling gates on a quantum device.

Expectation values provide the classical output of the quantum circuit. After the $H$--$R_Z$--CNOT gate sequence and measurement, the per-sensor Pauli-Z expectation is analytically computed as $\langle Z_i \rangle = \cos(\pi \cdot \mathrm{norm}_i)$, where $\mathrm{norm}_i \in [0,1]$ is the sensor value normalised against its physical operating bounds stored in `SENSOR_BOUNDS`. The composite weighted expectation $\langle Z \rangle_c = \sum_i w_i \langle Z_i \rangle$ (weights: temperature 0.30, vibration 0.30, humidity 0.15, pressure 0.15, energy 0.10) provides a single scalar risk indicator per machine that integrates all five sensor channels into a physically meaningful and interpretable risk score.

\subsection{Pauli-Z Multi-Sensor Risk Encoding (Classical Heuristic)}

The `PauliZRiskEncoder` class implements the core \emph{quantum-inspired} sensing component of QASAMAP. For each machine event, the encoder constructs a 5-qubit circuit \emph{in mathematical formulation} and returns a per-machine result dictionary containing the per-sensor $\langle Z_i \rangle$ values, the composite $\langle Z \rangle_c$, the risk label, the circuit depth, and the normalised sensor values used as circuit parameters. The \emph{actual runtime} computation evaluates Equation~\eqref{eq:composite_z} directly on the CPU without invoking Qiskit Aer or a quantum backend.

The risk label is determined by the following thresholds applied to $\langle Z \rangle_c$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item `QUANTUM-ANOMALY': $\langle Z \rangle_c < -0.20$ --- composite sensor state in the danger zone
    \item `QUANTUM-WARN': $-0.20 \leq \langle Z \rangle_c < 0.30$ --- composite state in the warning zone
    \item `QUANTUM-MARGINAL': $0.30 \leq \langle Z \rangle_c < 0.60$ --- composite state in the marginal zone
    \item `QUANTUM-NORMAL': $\langle Z \rangle_c \geq 0.60$ --- composite state in the safe zone
\end{itemize}

The key advantage of this encoding over classical threshold-based risk assessment is that $\langle Z \rangle_c$ integrates all five sensor channels simultaneously through the weighted sum of cosine-encoded expectations. A machine with, for example, three sensors slightly below their individual warning thresholds may produce a composite $\langle Z \rangle_c$ below $-0.20$ --- triggering a `QUANTUM-ANOMALY' label even though no individual sensor would trigger a classical warning. This sensitivity to sub-threshold multi-sensor risk combinations is the primary mechanism by which the heuristic layer expands machine coverage in the experimental evaluation.

\textbf{Algorithmic Disclosure.} The actual implementation in `classical_optimization.py` computes a normalized L2 distance from the training-set median per feature, passed through $1 - \exp(-3d)$. The resulting scalar is mapped to the four-tier `QUANTUM-*' label schema above. This design choice preserves the interpretability benefits of the Pauli-Z formulation while avoiding the overhead and noise of quantum circuit simulation.

\subsection{QUBO-Based Classical Priority Ranking}

The `QuantumAnnealingOptimizer' class formulates the maintenance prioritization problem as a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) problem. The QUBO objective minimizes the energy:
\begin{equation}
E(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T Q \mathbf{x} = -\sum_i w_i x_i + \frac{\lambda}{2} \sum_{i \neq j} x_i x_j
\label{eq:qubo}
\end{equation}
where $x_i \in \{0,1\}$ is the binary maintenance selection variable for machine $i$, $w_i$ is the machine's risk score from the Pauli-Z encoder, and $\lambda = 1.5$ is a penalty coefficient that discourages selecting all machines simultaneously. The diagonal terms $-w_i$ reward selecting high-risk machines (lower energy for high $w_i$ when $x_i = 1$), while the off-diagonal terms $+\lambda/2$ penalise over-selection (energy increases when multiple $x_i = 1$ simultaneously).

The QUBO is solved via \textbf{randomised local search} (not simulated annealing on a quantum processor): 200 samples of a greedy seed plus random 1-bit-flip local search, with deterministic behaviour given a fixed random seed. The resulting binary vector $\mathbf{x}^*$ identifies the selected machines, and a full priority ranking is produced by sorting all machines by descending risk score. The optimal QUBO energy is logged as a convergence quality measure.

\textbf{Algorithmic Disclosure.} The implementation in `classical_optimization.py' uses `random_constrained_search' with `n_samples=200' and `max_simultaneous=5'. It is \textbf{not} executed on a D-Wave annealer or any quantum hardware. The QUBO formulation is retained as a forward-compatible mathematical specification for future quantum hardware migration.

\subsection{Greedy Graph Scheduling Heuristic}

The `QAOAScheduler' class assigns all machines to maintenance time slots (Slot~A: IMMEDIATE/URGENT; Slot~B: PLANNED/ROUTINE) using a \textbf{greedy graph-message-passing heuristic} inspired by the Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm (QAOA). The scheduling problem is formulated as a MaxCut problem on a machine conflict graph in which adjacent machines (defined by sequential ordering) conflict if scheduled simultaneously. The problem Hamiltonian is:
\begin{equation}
H_P = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{(i,j) \in E} (I - Z_i Z_j) - \sum_i r_i \cdot 0.3 \cdot Z_i
\label{eq:qaoa_hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
where the first term maximises the cut (separating conflicting machines into different slots) and the second term provides Pauli-Z bias terms that steer high-risk machines (large $r_i$) toward Slot~A. The bias coefficient 0.3 is chosen to provide a strong but non-dominant influence that can be overcome by the MaxCut term when conflict constraints require high-risk machines to be separated across slots.

The \emph{classical} implementation performs three rounds of weighted-neighbour averaging on the existing T-GCN adjacency matrix, ranks nodes by their aggregated influence score, and assigns the top-ranked nodes to Slot~A subject to a maximum slot capacity. The bitstring interpretation (qubit $i$ in state $|0\rangle$ assigns machine $i$ to Slot~A, $|1\rangle$ to Slot~B) is preserved for conceptual consistency with the QAOA formulation.

\textbf{Algorithmic Disclosure.} The implementation in `classical_optimization.py' uses `graph_message_passing_score' with `n_rounds=3'. It is \textbf{not} a variational quantum circuit optimised via COBYLA. The QAOA Hamiltonian and ansatz are retained as a mathematical specification for future hardware implementation.

\subsection{Classical Override Mechanism}

The override mechanism is the integration point between the heuristic layer and the agentic pipeline. Implemented in the `QuantumOrchestrator' class, the mechanism evaluates three conditions for each machine: (1) its Pauli-Z label is `QUANTUM-ANOMALY' or `QUANTUM-WARN'; (2) it was selected by the QUBO local search; or (3) it was assigned to Slot~A with risk above 0.40. If any condition holds, the machine is escalated from passive monitoring to the full agentic reasoning chain, bypassing the classical `auto-LOW-skip' rule.

In the experimental evaluation, the override is triggered for 5 out of 7 machines (M-1, M-2, M-3, M-4, M-32, M-40). Machine~47, with $\langle Z \rangle_c = +0.3608$ and risk 0.3196, correctly remains in passive monitoring, demonstrating that the override mechanism applies principled selectivity rather than indiscriminately escalating all machines. The override reason is recorded in the `ContextMemoryStore' decision log for full traceability.

\subsection{Hardware Considerations and Future Quantum Migration}

All ``quantum'' circuits in the current QASAMAP implementation are \textbf{mathematical abstractions} executed as classical heuristics. The transition to physical quantum hardware (NISQ processors from IBM Quantum, Google Quantum AI, or IonQ) will require:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Replacing the `energy_distance_anomaly_score' with a genuine variational quantum classifier (VQC) or quantum kernel method.
    \item Replacing `random_constrained_search' with D-Wave quantum annealing or a classical-quantum hybrid QUBO solver.
    \item Replacing `graph_message_passing_score' with a QAOA circuit executed on a gate-based processor, using COBYLA or SPSA for variational parameter optimisation.
\end{enumerate}

Gate error rates on current NISQ hardware (typically $10^{-3}$--$10^{-2}$ for two-qubit gates) will introduce noise into the $\langle Z_i \rangle$ expectation values, potentially blurring the boundary between `QUANTUM-WARN' and `QUANTUM-MARGINAL' risk labels. Qubit decoherence limits the executable circuit depth; the 5-qubit Pauli-Z circuit with depth 3 (H, RZ, CNOT layers) is within the coherence budget of current superconducting processors, while a genuine QAOA circuit depth scales with the number of repetitions $p$ and may require error mitigation techniques such as zero-noise extrapolation for $p > 2$. The limited qubit connectivity of physical processors may also require additional SWAP gates to implement the linear CNOT chain, increasing circuit depth and error accumulation. These hardware-specific considerations constitute the primary motivation for the physical hardware validation identified as a future research direction in Chapter~7.
